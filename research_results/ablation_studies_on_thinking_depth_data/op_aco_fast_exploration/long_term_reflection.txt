The latest round of experiments—now encompassing Experiments #1 through #5 in this batch and building upon the foundational insights from prior work—further consolidates and sharpens the core principles of high-performance heuristic design for the Orienteering Problem (OP) within Ant Colony Optimization (ACO). The dominant three-pillar architecture remains unchallenged as the most effective framework: (1) an **unnormalized prize/distance ratio** as the foundational signal, (2) **adaptive exponentiation** modulated by global problem characteristics (density, sparsity, utilization), with **strict clipping bounds [0.02, 4.5]** emerging as empirically superior to [0.02, 5.0] in multiple trials, and (3) **dual soft feasibility constraints** based on return-to-depot slack, which consistently produce a beneficial 'squaring effect' in the probabilistic transition rule. Experiment #4’s score of 23.361—the highest observed to date—validates that parameter stability and simplicity outperform even well-intentioned adaptive mechanisms like dynamic clipping or sensitivity-based calibration.

A critical pattern now confirmed across all recent experiments is that **complexity degrades performance** unless it is strictly subordinate to the global structure. Attempts to introduce hierarchical pruning (#5), dynamic clipping (#4), skewness factors (#2, #5), or k-NN spatial signals (#1) either failed outright or underperformed the baseline three-pillar model. This reinforces the hierarchy of influence: **global problem characteristics set the desirability landscape**, while local or dynamic signals—when injected without algorithmic co-design—introduce noise rather than guidance. Notably, even the inclusion of skewness, once thought promising, has proven inconsistent and often detrimental, suggesting that the multiplicative combination of density, sparsity, and utilization already captures the essential structural variance of OP instances.

The **static-dynamic divide** remains the central bottleneck. No precomputed heuristic matrix can encode partial tour state or real-time remaining budget, and all attempts to approximate dynamic behavior within a static matrix (e.g., marginal gain estimates, residual budget fields, or k-NN prize density) have failed because the standard ACO transition rule lacks the scaffolding to interpret such signals. This limitation is not a flaw of the heuristic but of the interaction protocol between heuristic and algorithm. The consistent underperformance of hybrid schemes—even those using lightweight approximations—confirms that **breakthroughs require co-design**, not just better heuristics.

Methodologically, the most robust techniques are now clear: (a) **avoid normalization** of the base prize/distance ratio to preserve scale discriminability; (b) apply **tanh-based soft constraints with fixed offsets** (-1.0 for density, -2.0 for utilization) to maintain numerical stability; (c) use **dual identical feasibility filters** rather than single or hierarchical alternatives; and (d) **clip the combined adaptive exponent strictly within [0.02, 4.5]**, as deviations—even minor ones—degrade performance reliably. These settings define a narrow but high-performing operational regime for ACO on OP.

Future research priorities must shift decisively from heuristic engineering to **algorithm-heuristic alignment**. The top priority is now **minimal co-design**: implement a modified ACO construction loop where ants dynamically evaluate edge feasibility using real-time remaining budget, while retaining the static prize/distance backbone and global adaptive factors. This bridges the static-dynamic gap with minimal overhead and aligns with ACO’s incremental nature. Second, **validate scalability**: test the current best heuristic (Exp #4, score 23.361) on larger instances (n ≥ 200) to determine if dual constraints remain sufficient or if hierarchical decomposition becomes necessary—not in the heuristic, but in the ant construction process. Third, **explore learned blending cautiously**: if local signals are to be used, they should be simple (e.g., average prize of k-nearest neighbors) and weighted via lightweight regression on global instance features (e.g., prize variance, distance skewness), ensuring they never dominate the global structure.

In summary, the path forward is not toward smarter heuristics, but toward **smarter interactions**. The heuristic’s role is to provide a stable, globally-informed desirability landscape; the algorithm’s role must evolve to interpret that landscape contextually during construction. Alignment, not complexity, is the key to breakthrough performance.
