The synthesis of all prior experiments—including the newly analyzed Experiments #1 through #5—solidifies a paradigm shift in heuristic design for the Orienteering Problem under Ant Colony Optimization: **elite performance is achievable not through dynamic adaptation, but through static heuristics that act as high-fidelity proxies of dynamic desirability**. This insight has been repeatedly validated across multiple solution architectures, with scores now consistently exceeding 23 (e.g., Experiment #4’s 23.03 and Experiment #5’s 22.97), definitively disproving earlier assumptions about static heuristics hitting a performance ceiling.

A robust, generalizable design principle has emerged: **multiplicative fusion of four interlocking signals yields optimal results**: (1) a basic prize/distance efficiency term, (2) its squared (or higher-order) counterpart—leveraging mathematical redundancy as an implicit exponentiation mechanism within ACO’s probabilistic rule, (3) a lightweight budget feasibility proxy based on the i→j→depot return path (specifically, max(0, maxlen - distance[i,j] - distance[j,0])), and (4) a spatial clustering signal capturing local prize density. Critically, the form of this clustering matters: Gaussian-weighted or fixed-k nearest neighbor sums (k=8 or k=√n) outperform distance-threshold or inverse-distance variants, suggesting that smooth, locality-aware aggregation better aligns with ACO’s exploration dynamics.

This framework confirms that **numerical discriminative power and stability outweigh theoretical elegance**. Repeated failures of sigmoid feasibility, adaptive exponent tuning, and additive combinations underscore that ACO thrives on clear, multiplicative signals that avoid zero-probability traps and maintain gradient-like distinctions between good and bad moves. Moreover, the evaluation environment’s strict requirement for static n×n matrices renders any runtime state awareness impossible—making the design of *static proxies* the only viable path forward.

A major advance is the successful mitigation of scale sensitivity (50 vs. 100 nodes) through **instance-aware parameter modulation**, such as setting neighborhood size to k=√n, scaling Gaussian bandwidth by average inter-node distance, and normalizing components by problem-specific metrics (e.g., avg_distance). Experiment #5 demonstrated that such adaptations can boost 50-node performance by ~15% without sacrificing 100-node gains, narrowing—but not eliminating—the persistent performance gap. This suggests that while full scale-invariance remains elusive, it is partially addressable via feature-driven static adjustments.

However, fundamental limitations endure: **static heuristics cannot encode path-dependent opportunity costs** (e.g., visiting node j may block access to a high-value cluster due to budget exhaustion), and the assumption that return-to-depot cost equals distance[j,0] is often inaccurate in non-Euclidean or clustered topologies. These gaps explain why even the best static heuristics plateau around 23–23.2, hinting at a representational ceiling.

Updated research priorities now emphasize: (1) **Improving return-cost modeling** by replacing direct depot distance with k-hop shortest paths or landmark-based distance approximations to better estimate true remaining budget; (2) **Systematic exponent exploration** in the range [1.8, 2.5] for prize/distance terms, potentially combined with learned redundancy structures (e.g., cubic or hybrid exponents); (3) **Developing learned static heuristics**—training lightweight neural or regression models to predict optimal heuristic matrices from instance features (prize variance, node density, distance distribution), thereby bypassing manual formula design; and (4) **Hybrid proxy design** that integrates scale-adaptive parameters with topological awareness (e.g., using graph centrality or cluster detection to modulate neighborhood influence).

In summary, the frontier of OP heuristic design lies not in dynamics, but in **maximizing the fidelity of static proxies to the ideal dynamic decision function**. The next breakthrough will likely come from closing the gap between assumed and actual path feasibility—and from letting data, rather than intuition, dictate the structure of the static heuristic matrix.
