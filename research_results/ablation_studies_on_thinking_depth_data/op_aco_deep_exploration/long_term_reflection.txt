The cumulative research on heuristic design for the Orienteering Problem using Ant Colony Optimization has converged on a clear set of principles that balance simplicity, locality, and structured lookahead. The consistent outperformance of the fixed 0.6/0.4 weighted depth-2 lookahead combined with MDS-based geometric guidance—despite numerous attempts to replace or adapt it—confirms that *stable, well-tuned static heuristics* are more effective than dynamically adaptive or ensemble-based alternatives. This reinforces a core insight: because the heuristic matrix is computed once before ant construction, it cannot incorporate path-dependent state (e.g., remaining budget), and thus must encode *generalized, instance-level guidance* rather than context-sensitive decisions.

New experiments further validate that the prize-to-distance-squared ratio (prize[j]/distance[i][j]^2) remains the strongest baseline signal, providing essential locality bias that prevents ants from overcommitting to distant, low-density regions. Attempts to replace this with multiplicative fusions (Experiment #6) or learned ensembles (Experiment #7) consistently underperformed, revealing that additive combination with fixed weights preserves component independence and avoids destructive interference. Moreover, the failure of Laplacian eigenmaps (Experiments #1, #3) to match MDS—even when prize-weighted—suggests that MDS’s ability to preserve global pairwise distances in a low-dimensional embedding provides uniquely valuable directional cues for tour construction, particularly in larger (100-node) instances.

A critical pattern emerging across all experiments is the *robustness-performance trade-off*: simpler heuristics (e.g., fixed weights, MDS + depth-2 lookahead) yield higher and more stable scores, while complex adaptations (adaptive blending, k-step lookahead, learned weights) introduce noise, fragility, or computational overhead without commensurate gains. Notably, even small deviations—such as changing the budget penalty coefficient from -2.0 to -2.5 or -1.5—degrade performance, underscoring the sensitivity of the system to parameter tuning. This implies that future progress may lie not in architectural novelty, but in *precision engineering* of existing components.

Persistent challenges remain around *problem-size robustness*. While the best hybrid heuristics excel on 100-node instances, they often lag slightly on 50-node problems, suggesting that geometric guidance and lookahead are less critical in smaller, denser graphs where local greediness suffices. This points to a need for *scale-aware heuristic composition*—not through dynamic per-edge logic, but via precomputed instance features (e.g., node count, average distance, prize variance) that modulate a small set of hyperparameters (e.g., lookahead weight, sparsification rate, penalty strength).

Promising future directions now include: (a) conducting ablation studies to isolate the marginal contribution of each component (MDS alignment, depth-2 lookahead, budget penalty, sparsification) and identify minimal high-performing subsets; (b) exploring *alternative geometric embeddings* (e.g., UMAP, t-SNE) that may offer MDS-like directionality with better numerical stability or scalability; (c) refining the depth-2 lookahead to include *return-to-depot feasibility* in its reward estimation, thereby better respecting the tour-length constraint; (d) developing *instance-adaptive hyperparameter selection*—not for heuristic blending, but for tuning fixed parameters like the budget penalty coefficient or neighbor pruning thresholds based on global metrics; and (e) investigating whether *offline meta-learning* can predict optimal static configurations (e.g., MDS vs no MDS, lookahead depth) from instance fingerprints, sidestepping online complexity.

In summary, the path forward lies in disciplined refinement rather than radical innovation: preserving the proven core of prize/distance² + MDS + fixed-weight depth-2 lookahead + exp(-2·distance/maxlen) penalty, while carefully optimizing its parameters and preprocessing steps for robustness across problem scales. The overarching principle is clear: in static heuristic design for OP, *less is more*—but only when every component is precisely calibrated.
