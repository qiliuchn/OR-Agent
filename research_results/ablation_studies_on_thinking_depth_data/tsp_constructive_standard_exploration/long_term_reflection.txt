The cumulative research on constructive heuristics for the Traveling Salesman Problem has solidified a nuanced and empirically grounded understanding of what is achievable within the constrained `select_next_node` API. Recent experiments—particularly Experiment #5’s deceptively high-performing but fundamentally non-functional error-correction attempt—have reinforced core principles while exposing critical implementation pitfalls. The performance ceiling remains firmly anchored between ~6.26 and ~6.30, with the best scores (e.g., 6.263) arising not from novel feedback mechanisms, but from refined execution of the established 'minimalist, stable core': hybrid cost estimation combining immediate edge cost with a realistic future-cost surrogate (typically greedy nearest-neighbor completion), evaluated over a small fixed candidate pool (k=5–8), and crucially, **ignoring the misleading `destination_node` parameter in practice**, even if it appears in intermediate calculations.

Key patterns are now unequivocally validated: (1) **Simulated post-construction effects (e.g., fixed 2-opt improvement factors like 0.92) consistently outperform structural bounds like MST**, which lack correlation with actual completion costs in metric TSP; (2) **Candidate restriction to top-k nearest neighbors is essential for stability and speed**, with k=7 emerging as a robust default; (3) **Adaptive or dynamic weighting schemes introduce fragility without meaningful gains**, whereas fixed or mildly adaptive blends (e.g., 80% NN, 20% structural) suffice; and (4) **Attempts to inject stateful feedback (e.g., error correction via running averages) are structurally impossible** within the stateless, per-call API—any apparent success stems from the underlying estimator, not the feedback loop.

Notably, Experiment #5’s score of 6.263—while matching the theoretical ceiling—was achieved *despite* its intended innovation, not because of it. This underscores a critical methodological insight: **performance metrics can be misleading if the causal mechanism is misunderstood**. The true driver was the hybrid estimator’s quality, not the non-updated error correction term. This reinforces the need for rigorous ablation studies and cautions against overinterpreting score improvements without mechanistic validation.

Persistent challenges remain rooted in the API’s design: irreversible early decisions, compounding myopia in larger instances (100-city scores consistently >8.5), and the absence of tour history prevent geometric reasoning or backtracking. These are not solvable through heuristic tweaks alone. Instead, the evidence overwhelmingly supports a **two-stage paradigm**: `select_next_node` should function as a fast, reliable seed generator, with mandatory post-construction refinement (e.g., 2-opt) applied externally. This shifts the research goal from 'optimal construction' to 'optimal seeding.'

Future directions must therefore prioritize: (1) **Validating the two-stage hypothesis** by integrating mandatory 2-opt into the evaluation pipeline to measure end-to-end performance; (2) **Optimizing the hybrid estimator for speed and scalability**, potentially replacing full greedy simulation with learned or precomputed surrogates that preserve the calibrated optimism of factors like 0.92; (3) **Formalizing symmetric cycle modeling** that explicitly decouples from `destination_node`, treating all nodes as part of a closed loop; and (4) **Exploring minimal external state only if API constraints can be relaxed**, though current evidence suggests even this offers limited upside. The path forward is not in making construction smarter, but in making it faster and more compatible with downstream refinement—accepting that `select_next_node`’s role is to enable, not to solve.
