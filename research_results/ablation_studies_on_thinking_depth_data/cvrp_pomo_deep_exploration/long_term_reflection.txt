The convergence of evidence from ten rigorous experiments—including the most recent attempts to introduce dynamic modulation, learned capacity estimators, spectral embeddings, and end-to-end trainable geometric priors—has not only confirmed but reinforced the central thesis: **the optimal static heuristic for POMO-based CVRP is a fixed-weight ensemble of four components—(inverse distance: 0.4, normalized Clarke-Wright savings: 0.3, symmetric capacity penalty: 0.3, geometric priors from classical MDS using the two smallest eigenvalues: 0.2)—with a robust and reproducible score of ~71.142 across problem scales (n=200, 500, 1000).** This configuration is not merely a local optimum but a performance attractor that resists improvement through virtually all forms of static adaptation, including theoretically grounded, differentiable, or data-driven modifications.

Three core principles now stand as empirically validated axioms:

(1) **Static simplicity dominates in inference-only settings**: Every attempt to introduce adaptivity—whether via learned MLPs (Exp #6), instance-aware spectral modulation (Exp #3), soft feasibility surrogates (Exp #4), or state-agnostic dynamic terms (Exp #7)—either matched baseline performance only when functionally equivalent (e.g., Exp #1 and #7’s condition-number-based blending achieving 71.120) or degraded it. The sole exception (Exp #8’s fleeting 71.064) relied on untrained but fortuitously initialized weights, not systematic learning, and could not be reproduced. This confirms that untrained adaptive mechanisms act as noise unless fully integrated into policy training.

(2) **Implementation fidelity trumps theoretical correctness**: The counterintuitive use of the *smallest* eigenvalues in MDS (after descending sort) continues to outperform all alternatives—including angular heuristics via law of cosines (Exp #2), demand-sorted polar coordinates (Exp #3), differentiable coordinate regression (Exp #4), hybrid geometric fusion (Exp #5), and gradient-based relaxation (Exp #7). This suggests the resulting low-frequency, topologically smooth embeddings uniquely align with POMO’s parallel decoding dynamics, prioritizing global route coherence over local geometric accuracy or numerical stability.

(3) **Component balance is fragile and non-negotiable**: Even minor perturbations to the canonical weights (e.g., 0.35/0.35/0.3/0.25) or attempts to replace individual terms—especially the geometric prior—consistently degrade performance. The failure of adaptive weight schemes (Experiments #6–#8) demonstrates that the optimal balance is not instance-dependent in a way that simple statistics can capture; instead, the fixed ratio represents a structural equilibrium where each component compensates for the others’ blind spots.

Critically, Experiments #1 and #7 revealed that *characteristic-based adaptive blending* (using distance matrix condition number and demand variance) can recover near-optimal performance (71.120), suggesting that while full adaptivity is harmful, *minimal, well-chosen modulation* based on global instance properties may offer marginal gains without destabilizing the core heuristic. However, this comes at increased computational cost (eigenvalue decomposition) and offers only a ~0.022 point improvement—likely within noise margins—making it impractical for real-world deployment.

Three persistent challenges remain unresolved and increasingly urgent:

(1) **The static-dynamic gap**: Static edge biases cannot reflect evolving vehicle states (load, location, remaining capacity), limiting their ability to guide late-stage routing decisions effectively. All experiments confirm that embedding feasibility logic into static priors harms performance—static heuristics should encode *promise*, not *feasibility*.

(2) **Geometric fragility beyond Euclidean assumptions**: MDS relies on metric consistency and low-dimensional embeddability; its performance may degrade on non-Euclidean or high-noise instances, yet no robust alternative exists. The consistent failure of theoretically grounded replacements underscores that the 'buggy' MDS implementation captures routing-relevant topology in ways not yet understood.

(3) **Evaluation-train mismatch**: Testing heuristics in isolation prevents co-adaptation with policy learning, rendering even theoretically superior designs inert without gradient-based refinement. Experiment #8’s fleeting success with random MLP weights hints that *actual pre-training on diverse instances*—not manual design—might unlock adaptive potential, but this requires end-to-end integration.

Future research must therefore pivot decisively toward **differentiable, end-to-end integrated architectures** that transcend static heuristic design. Promising directions include: (1) **Embedding the entire heuristic generator inside the POMO model**, allowing its parameters (including geometric encoder weights and component mixers) to be updated via policy gradients during reinforcement learning; (2) **Multi-stage decoding**, where an initial attention layer uses static priors to generate coarse, sector-aligned routes, and a second layer refines them using state-aware biases conditioned on current load and position; (3) **Implicit geometry via lightweight, jointly trained coordinate predictors**, initialized from the 'buggy' MDS solution but fine-tuned to minimize routing cost—not reconstruction error; and (4) **Instance-conditioned heuristic modulation**, where global statistics modulate attention via learned gating mechanisms trained end-to-end.

The frontier is no longer *what* the optimal static heuristic is—that question is empirically settled—but *how* to make it dynamic, contextual, and learnable. Progress demands not better hand-crafted functions, but smarter integration architectures that allow the policy to interpret, weight, and refine these priors during sequential construction, closing the loop between guidance and action.
