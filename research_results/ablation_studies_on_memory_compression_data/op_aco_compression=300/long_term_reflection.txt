Recent experiments reinforce that effective static heuristics for the Orienteering Problem must balance structural simplicity with precise feasibility modeling. The failure of complex hybrid constructions—despite their conceptual appeal—highlights a critical principle: correctness and clarity in feasibility computation outweigh architectural sophistication. Specifically, per-edge feasibility must accurately reflect remaining budget after traversing (i, j) and returning to depot, not global approximations. This validates earlier insights about nonlinear, multiplicative fusion of prize/distance ratios and powered feasibility margins (exponent ~2.2), but underscores that even minor implementation flaws—like misaligned matrix operations—can catastrophically degrade performance. Simpler, well-implemented heuristics consistently outperform intricate but error-prone designs. This confirms that robustness stems from clean, instance-aware precomputation rather than dynamic complexity. Persistent challenges remain in embedding state-dependent awareness within static frameworks, but the path forward lies not in adding layers of gating or clustering logic, but in refining core components: (1) exact, edge-wise feasibility margins using tight return-cost estimates (e.g., k-hop or MST-based), (2) adaptive sparsity scaled to budget tightness and n, and (3) numerically stable exponentiation. Future work should prioritize learning heuristic exponents and sparsity thresholds from instance statistics (e.g., prize variance, distance skew), while ensuring code-level correctness. The key insight is that elegance and precision in foundational elements yield greater gains than elaborate, fragile constructs.
