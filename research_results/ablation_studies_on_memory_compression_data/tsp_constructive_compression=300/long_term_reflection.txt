The latest experiment reinforces that diversity-aware candidate selection—when carefully calibrated—enhances constructive TSP heuristics by balancing proximity with spatial dispersion, validating the principle that modestly wider and more representative local exploration improves solution quality. The success of inverse-distance-based repulsion over max-min diversity suggests that smooth, differentiable penalties align better with greedy completion estimation than discrete dispersion metrics. This further confirms that reliability and empirical grounding outweigh theoretical elegance in bounded-horizon decision-making. Notably, performance gains were most pronounced on larger instances (100 cities), while medium-sized problems (50 cities) remain challenging, exposing a critical gap: fixed hyperparameters fail to generalize across scales. This underscores the need for instance-adaptive mechanisms that adjust diversity weights, candidate pool sizes, and structural penalties based on problem-specific features like density or diameter. The persistent lack of path history awareness continues to limit dynamic adaptation, reinforcing the call for interfaces that expose partial tour structure. Future work must prioritize (1) lightweight, rollout-trained estimators using coordinate-free topological features, (2) adaptive parameterization tied to instance characteristics, (3) hybrid completion strategies blending greedy steps with micro-local search, and (4) incremental refinement frameworks that leverage historical context. The overarching insight sharpens: high-performing constructive heuristics emerge not from complex models, but from consistent, empirically calibrated decision rules operating within intelligently expanded local neighborhoods.
