The latest experiments reinforce and refine the core principles governing effective static heuristics for ACO in the Orienteering Problem. The three-component multiplicative structure—prize-weighted distance decay, return-to-depot feasibility, and local prize density—remains empirically dominant, with even minor deviations (e.g., graph-based prize propagation) degrading performance. This confirms that heuristic components must be simple, interpretable, and synergistically aligned with ACO’s row-wise probabilistic decisions. Budget-normalized distance scaling (via maxlen/n) is not merely beneficial but essential for cross-instance generalization, acting as a scale-invariant anchor that enables consistent edge comparisons across problem sizes. Mean-relative sparsification at ~15% of the positive mean now emerges as the optimal structural filter, outperforming quantile or degree-based alternatives by preserving high-signal edges while reducing noise—demonstrating that ACO thrives on sparse yet informative guidance rather than dense, noisy matrices. Crucially, all attempts to enhance static heuristics beyond this scaffold hit a performance ceiling near 23.0, underscoring their fundamental limitation: they lack awareness of dynamic states like remaining budget or partial tour composition. This validates the separation-of-concerns paradigm—static heuristics should encode 'what is generally valuable,' while runtime mechanisms must handle 'what is currently feasible.' Future progress hinges on lightweight, analytically grounded dynamic augmentations that rescale or reweight edges based on real-time constraints without disrupting the base signal. Promising directions include using geometric proximity to adapt sparsification thresholds locally, or applying feasibility-aware bounds derived from triangle inequality to modulate edge desirability during construction. Any such mechanism must remain computationally cheap and provably non-destabilizing to ACO’s convergence. Simplicity, scale invariance, and strict adherence to ACO’s decision logic remain non-negotiable; complexity without clear mechanistic justification consistently harms performance. The path forward is not richer static models, but smarter, minimal dynamic overlays.
