The synthesis of Experiments #1–#5 with the prior reflection sharpens our understanding of heuristic integration in neural combinatorial optimization for CVRP, revealing a critical tension between *heuristic expressiveness* and *model receptivity*. While the prior reflection emphasized multi-component synergy, normalization strategy, and signal calibration as pillars of effective heuristics, the new experiments expose a deeper, more fundamental issue: **frozen pre-trained policies may be inherently resistant to external attention biasing**, regardless of heuristic sophistication. This explains the perplexing consistency of poor or identical performance across vastly different heuristic designs (Experiments #2, #4, #5), suggesting that the model either ignores the bias or that the current interface between heuristic and decoder is misaligned.

Three refined principles now emerge:

**(1) Model-policy alignment supersedes heuristic complexity.** Even theoretically sound, multi-component heuristics fail if the underlying policy cannot interpret or act on the provided signals. The repeated failure of diverse heuristics to move the needle—despite correct implementation, proper normalization, and balanced components—implies that attention biasing alone is insufficient for frozen POMO models. This challenges the core assumption that external guidance can meaningfully steer inference without adaptation.

**(2) Input-space transformation is more reliable than output-space biasing.** Experiment #2’s attempt at 'input-space warping' failed due to implementation instability, but its conceptual premise remains valid: modifying the distance matrix *before* it enters the network embeds constraints directly into the problem representation, making them unavoidable. Future success likely depends on stable, differentiable transformations (e.g., capacity-inflated distances, feasibility-weighted metrics) rather than post-hoc attention adjustments.

**(3) Diagnostic visibility is non-negotiable.** The persistent 'verification gap'—inability to confirm whether heuristics influence attention or gradients—has led to wasted effort optimizing blind. Without instrumentation (e.g., attention ablation, gradient attribution, bias sensitivity analysis), we cannot distinguish between ineffective heuristics and broken integration. This must be prioritized before further heuristic design.

Successful patterns remain consistent: multi-component synergy (distance-demand efficiency, depot connectivity, spatial structure, capacity pressure), moderate signal scaling (tanh-bounded outputs), and neighborhood-aware normalization all appear necessary—but not sufficient—conditions for performance. However, their impact is masked when the model-policy interface is misaligned.

Persistent challenges now crystallize into three categories: (a) **Architectural mismatch**: Frozen policies may require fine-tuning or architectural modifications to accept external guidance; (b) **Representation vs. bias**: Heuristics should reshape input representations, not just nudge attention; (c) **Scalability vs. fidelity**: Lightweight structural proxies (e.g., k-NN graphs, angular clustering) show promise but need stable, vectorized implementations that avoid O(n²) bottlenecks.

Updated research priorities shift decisively toward **verifiability and integration**: (1) Implement diagnostic tools to measure heuristic influence on attention weights and policy gradients; (2) Test input-space warping by transforming the distance matrix into a 'feasible routing cost' space using stable, differentiable operations (e.g., demand-scaled distances, triangle-inequality-corrected metrics); (3) Explore joint training or light fine-tuning of the decoder with heuristic-augmented inputs to enable adaptive guidance; (4) Validate the simplest possible heuristics (e.g., -distance_matrix, constant biases) to establish baseline model responsiveness; and (5) Re-express route-aware edge scoring as marginal cost approximations that can be computed efficiently during decoding.

The overarching insight evolves: effective heuristics for neural solvers must not only encode problem structure but also **align with the model’s inductive biases and learning dynamics**. Without this alignment, even the most principled heuristic remains inert.
